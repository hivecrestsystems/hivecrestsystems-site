**The short answer:** AI and automation help most with repetitive, rule-based back-office work: copying data between apps, reminders, first drafts and routine questions. The software is cheap, about $20–$25 per person a month for an AI assistant and $0 to about $70 a month for a tool that connects your apps. Paying someone to build automations costs more, typically $3,000–$15,000 for a small project.

The bigger cost is your time: setting tools up and checking their work. Results also vary because "using AI" can mean one person drafting emails or a system that answers your phones. This guide covers which tasks to automate first, what tools and setup cost, what to keep a person on, how to start small and measure the result, and what the evidence says about the payoff.

This guide is general information, not legal or tax advice. Check the rules it mentions with an attorney or a tax professional, and confirm current figures with the official source, because both the rules and the prices change.

## At a glance

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| What to automate first | Back-office tasks that repeat every week, follow a clear rule and are cheap to get wrong |
| AI assistant for staff | $20–$25 per user a month on a business plan |
| Tool that connects your apps | $0 to about $70 a month at small volumes |
| AI phone answering | $129–$500 a month for about 300 calls |
| Paying someone to set it up | $60–$300 an hour, or $2,000–$15,000 a project |

| Does it pay off | A 14% productivity gain in one large study of support agents; about 5% of corporate pilots showed fast revenue gains in MIT research |

## Which tasks to automate first

Start with back-office work that repeats every week and follows a rule. MIT research on corporate AI projects found the biggest returns there, even though more than half of AI budgets went to sales and marketing tools ([Fortune][fortune]).

### A simple test for any task

A task is a good first candidate when all four of these are true:

- It happens often, at least weekly.
- It follows a rule a new hire could learn in a day.
- A mistake is cheap and easy to spot.
- The information already sits in software, not on paper or in someone's head.

### Candidates, roughly in order

| Task | Why it suits automation | Kind of tool | Risk if it goes wrong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Copying data between apps | Pure rule, no judgment: a web form into your customer list, an order into your books | A tool that connects apps | Low |
| Booking, confirmations and reminders | Follows a rule and cuts no-shows | Scheduling software | Low |
| Invoice reminders and receipt capture | Repeats monthly and is easy to check | Your accounting software's built-in features | Low |
| Meeting notes and summaries | A person can skim the result | An AI assistant or meeting tool | Low |
| First drafts of emails, posts, product descriptions and job ads | A fast start that a person edits before sending | An AI assistant | Low, if a person reviews it |
| Sorting and tagging incoming email or leads | Mostly rules, with some judgment | An AI step inside an automation | Medium |
| After-hours calls and messages | A missed call can be a lost job | AI phone answering | Medium |
| Website chat for common questions | Many repeat questions | A chatbot trained on your own pages | Medium to high (see [what to keep a person on](#what-to-keep-a-person-on)) |
| Quotes, refunds and anything that commits the business | Looks repetitive, but errors cost money | AI drafts it; a person approves | High |

The order is a judgment call, based on the back-office finding above and a large study in which an AI assistant helped support agents rather than replacing them (see [what the evidence says](#what-the-evidence-says-about-results)).

### Two habits that matter more than the tool

- **Fix the process first.** Automating a messy process gives you the same mess, faster. Write the steps down before you buy anything.
- **Use what you already pay for.** Accounting, scheduling, email and office software now include automation and AI features. Check those before adding a subscription.

## What AI and automation cost

A three-person business can give everyone an AI assistant and run one automation tool for under $1,000 a year. Hiring someone to build the automations costs more than the software. The prices below were checked on November 19, 2026. They change often, so check the vendor's live page before you buy.

### AI assistants for your staff

Plans differ in how they treat what your staff type in, as well as in price.

### Paying someone to set it up

- **Freelancer using no-code tools:** $60–$100 an hour ([TechConcepts][techconcepts]).
- **Freelance developer writing custom code:** $100–$160 an hour ([TechConcepts][techconcepts]).
- **Consultant or agency:** $100–$300 an hour ([Layer3 Labs][layer3-agency]).
- **One automated workflow, built for you:** $2,000–$8,000 ([Groovy Web][groovy]), or $2,000–$12,000 by another estimate ([Layer3 Labs][layer3-agency]).
- **A typical small business project:** $3,000–$15,000 to build, plus $200–$600 a month in tools ([Builts AI][builts]).
- **Software that acts on its own across several systems (often called AI agents):** $25,000–$60,000+ ([Groovy Web][groovy]). At that point it's a software project, priced closer to [building an app](/guides/app-development-cost).

These setup figures come from firms that sell these services, so read them as asking prices rather than independent benchmarks. In Maryland, expect sales tax on top (see [Maryland rules](#maryland-rules-that-apply)).

### Two worked examples

These are arithmetic on the prices above, not quotes.

- **Doing it yourself, three people:** three seats on a business AI assistant plan at $20 a user, billed yearly, come to 3 × $20 × 12 = $720. ChatGPT Business and Claude Team both list that price. A connecting tool at Zapier Professional's starting price adds $19.99 × 12 = $239.88. Total: about $960 a year.
- **Paying for one build:** say a task takes 5 hours a week of staff time at $25 an hour. That's $125 a week, or $6,500 a year. A $2,000 build, the low end of the range for one workflow, plus a $20-a-month tool costs $2,240 in the first year. It pays for itself in about four months, but only if it really removes all five hours.

### Where the bills grow

- **Usage, not seats:** a 20-step automation that runs 500 times a month uses 10,000 credits, a whole entry plan ([Coworker][coworker]).
- **Checking too often:** an automation that checks for new data every minute uses 43,200 credits a month before it does any work ([eesel][eesel]).
- **Running out:** when you hit your limit on Zapier, automations pause or switch to a higher pay-per-task rate. On Make, they stop ([Zapier][zapier]; [Make][make]).
- **Phone minutes:** per-minute and per-call plans cost the most in your busiest month.

Set usage alerts and a monthly ceiling wherever the tool allows it.

## What makes a project cost more or less

Cost follows the number of apps being connected and how much judgment the task needs. A rule between two apps is cheap. An automation that reads, sorts or decides across several systems is not.

| Driver | Costs more | Costs less |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of systems | Each extra app adds setup, testing and one more thing that can break | Two mainstream apps with ready-made connectors |
| Judgment | AI steps that read, sort or decide | Fixed rules: when this happens, do that |
| Who builds it | An agency or consultant | A staff member using a template |
| Data quality | Duplicates, missing fields, records kept on paper | Clean records already in software |
| Cost of an error | Customer-facing or money-moving work, which needs review steps | Internal tasks that are easy to check |
| Upkeep | Many automations nobody documented | A short list with a named owner |

## What to keep a person on

Keep a person on anything that makes a promise, spends money, judges a person or needs a license. Your business answers for what its tools say and do. When the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) announced a set of AI cases on September 25, 2024, its chair said existing laws have no exemption for AI ([FTC][ftc-ai]).

| Keep a person on | Why | Rule or case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What a chatbot tells customers about prices, refunds or policy | You can be held to it | A Canadian tribunal held Air Canada liable for its chatbot's wrong answer about a fare policy, rejecting the argument that the chatbot was responsible for itself ([American Bar Association][aba]). It isn't US precedent |
| Reviews and testimonials | Fake reviews are banned, including ones written by AI | The FTC's rule, announced August 14, 2024, allows civil penalties for knowing violations ([FTC][ftc-reviews]) |
| Outbound calls that use an AI voice | Federal robocall law treats them as artificial-voice calls, which come with consent rules | An FCC ruling in 2024 ([FCC][fcc]) |
| Hiring decisions | Discrimination law still applies when software helps decide | Maryland limits face scanning in job interviews ([Md. Labor and Employment § 3-717][md-3-717]) |
| Legal, tax, medical or financial advice | It needs a licensed person | The FTC acted against a company marketed as a robot lawyer ([FTC][ftc-ai]) |
| Paying bills, refunds and payroll | Errors move real money and are hard to reverse | General practice |
| Upset customers and complaints | Judgment and goodwill matter more than speed | General practice |

A workable rule: AI drafts, sorts and reminds; a person approves, sends and signs. Give every chatbot and phone system a clear way to reach a person.

### Three risks to plan for

- **Wrong answers:** AI text can state wrong facts with confidence. Have a person check prices, policies and facts before anything is published or sent, and keep a log of chatbot conversations.
- **Customer data:** what staff paste into a personal AI account may be used for training. On Claude's Free, Pro and Max plans, training applies unless the user opts out, while Team plans don't train by default ([Anthropic][anthropic]). Use business plans, check each vendor's setting, and give staff a short written rule on what not to paste.
- **Lock-in:** if your builder holds the accounts, you lose the automations when you part ways. If nothing is written down, no one can fix them when an app changes. Keep every account in your business's name, with the builder added as a user, and get a one-page description of each automation and who owns it.

## How to start small and measure whether it worked

Pick one task, measure it, and run the automation beside the old way before you rely on it. Most small automations take hours to days to set up, not months.

- **Pick one task** that passes the [four-part test](#a-simple-test-for-any-task), and write its steps down.
- **Time it first.** Note how long the task takes each week. Without that baseline, you can't tell whether the automation worked.
- **Run both for two weeks.** Keep the manual process going beside the automation, and compare the results before you rely on it.
- **If you hire help, start with a small paid trial,** at a fixed price for a defined result, with one number you both agree to measure.
- **Count the hours it actually removed.** The payback in the [worked example](#two-worked-examples) only happens if the work really goes away.

| Step | Typical time | Basis |

## What the evidence says about results

The best evidence shows real but modest gains on specific tasks, and many projects that produce nothing measurable. Almost none of it comes from businesses with fewer than 20 people.

| Finding | Detail | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An AI assistant raised output 14% | In a study of 5,179 customer support agents, published as an NBER working paper, issues resolved per hour rose 14% on average ([NBER][nber]) | One large company's support team |
| Newer staff gained the most | Novice and lower-skilled agents improved 34%; experienced agents barely changed ([NBER][nber]) | The same study |
| Most corporate pilots showed little measurable effect | In an MIT report, about 5% of generative AI pilots produced fast revenue gains; the rest had little or no measurable effect on profit ([Fortune][fortune]) | Large companies; based on 150 interviews, a survey of 350 employees and 300 public AI projects |
| Buying beat building | In the same report, tools bought from specialist vendors succeeded about 67% of the time, and in-house builds only a third as often ([Fortune][fortune]) | The article doesn't explain how success was measured |
| Small firms using AI were hiring | In a US Chamber of Commerce survey, 82% of small businesses using AI grew their workforce in the past year ([US Chamber][uschamber]) | A survey; growing firms may simply adopt more |

What this means for a small business:

- **The gains are task-sized.** A 14% lift on one kind of work is useful. It isn't a reason to expect a smaller payroll.
- **Help for newer staff is the clearest benefit.** In the support study, the tool spread the habits of the best agents to the rest ([NBER][nber]), which points to training and consistency as much as speed.
- **Vague goals usually fail.** The MIT authors blamed how the tools were fitted to the work, not the quality of the AI ([Fortune][fortune]). Many "hours saved" figures online come from the company selling the tool.

## How many small businesses use AI

The answer runs from under 20% to 76%, depending on who asks and how. The federal government's survey gives the lowest figures; surveys by business groups and banks give the highest.

| Survey | Who was asked | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Census Bureau, Business Trends and Outlook Survey | US businesses of all sizes, about AI use in the past two weeks | 17–20% used AI from December 14, 2025 to May 3, 2026. By size, 37% of firms with 250 or more employees did, and under 20% of firms with four or fewer ([Census Bureau][census]) |
| Federal Reserve, Small Business Credit Survey | Small employer firms | 46% use AI, and 15% plan to within a year, as reported by [Delaware Inc.][delaware] |
| US Chamber of Commerce | Small businesses, about generative AI (tools that write text or create images) | 58% in 2025, up from 40% in 2024 and 23% in 2023 ([US Chamber][uschamber]) |
| Goldman Sachs 10,000 Small Businesses Voices | 1,256 owners in Goldman's own program, January 27–February 4, 2026 | 76% use AI, but only 14% say it is fully part of their core operations, as reported by [Sagnik Bhattacharya][sagnik] |

The gap comes from the question and the sample. Census asks about the past two weeks, and until November 2025 it counted only AI used to produce goods or services, which left out jobs like drafting emails ([Census Bureau][census]). The Chamber and Goldman Sachs survey their own audiences, which likely lean toward more engaged owners. In the Census survey, use grew over the first months of 2026 among firms with at least 20 employees, but didn't change significantly among smaller ones ([Census Bureau][census]).

## Red flags when you're buying AI help

The loudest warning sign is a promise of guaranteed income or a replaced workforce. In September 2024, the FTC announced five cases in one sweep against deceptive AI claims: a service marketed as a robot lawyer, three schemes that promised passive income from AI-powered online stores, and a writing tool that could generate unlimited customer reviews ([FTC][ftc-ai]).

| Red flag | Why it matters | What to look for instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Guaranteed" revenue, savings or hours | No one can promise results from your data and your staff, and the FTC has pursued AI income claims ([FTC][ftc-ai]) | A small paid trial with a number you both agree to measure |
| A pitch to replace your receptionist, bookkeeper or marketer | The evidence shows help on tasks, not replaced roles | A list of the specific tasks to be automated |
| A custom build when an off-the-shelf tool exists | In the MIT research, bought tools succeeded far more often than in-house builds ([Fortune][fortune]) | Proof the provider checked ready-made options first |
| Open-ended hourly billing | Hourly work gives the least budget certainty ([Layer3 Labs][layer3-agency]) | A fixed price for a defined result |
| Accounts in the builder's name, and no handover notes | You can't change or fix what you paid for | Accounts in your name, and a one-page description of each automation |
| A chatbot with no route to a person | You answer for what it says | A visible way to reach a person, and logged conversations |
| An offer to write or post reviews | The FTC's rule bans fake reviews ([FTC][ftc-reviews]) | Asking real customers for reviews |

## Maryland rules that apply

Four Maryland rules touch a small business using AI: the 3% sales tax on IT services, the state privacy law, a hiring rule on face scanning, and a 2026 law on prices set from personal data.

| Rule | What it says | Who it affects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3% sales tax on IT and data services | Since July 1, 2025, taxable services include custom programming, data processing, and the design and development of robotic process automation software, meaning software that repeats routine computer tasks ([Comptroller of Maryland][md-tax]) | If you pay someone to build automations, expect 3% on the invoice. Ask an accountant how a given subscription is taxed |
| Maryland Online Data Privacy Act | Applies to a business that handled the personal data of at least 35,000 Maryland consumers in the past year, or 10,000 if more than 20% of its revenue comes from selling personal data. In effect since October 1, 2025, and applies to data handling from April 1, 2026 ([Bass, Berry & Sims][bassberry]; [Nemphos Braue][nemphos]) | Most very small businesses fall under the threshold. Online stores and apps with large customer lists may not |
| Face scanning in job interviews | An employer may not use facial recognition to create a facial template of an applicant during an interview unless the applicant signs a waiver giving their name, the date, their consent, and whether they read the waiver ([Md. Labor and Employment § 3-717][md-3-717]) | Any employer using video-interview software with face analysis |
| Protection From Predatory Pricing Act (HB 895) | Signed April 28, 2026, and in effect since October 1, 2026. Grocery stores and food delivery services may not use personal data to charge individual shoppers more; the Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division enforces it ([Morgan Lewis][morganlewis]; [Ballard Spahr via Mondaq][mondaq]) | Food retailers and delivery platforms. One of these law firms also reads it as requiring other merchants to disclose prices set by algorithm or from personal data ([Morgan Lewis][morganlewis]; [Ballard Spahr via Mondaq][mondaq]), so confirm with an attorney |

The privacy law's bar is low by national standards: other states mostly start at 100,000 consumers ([Enzuzo][enzuzo]), and a regional online seller can reach 35,000 Maryland customers. If you're over the threshold, know which AI tools receive your customer data. For more on the tax, see [Maryland's 3% tax on IT and software services](/guides/maryland-tax-on-it-services).
